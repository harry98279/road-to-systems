
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
import numpy as np 

X = np.array([[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]])
y = np.array([2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 8.1, 9.8, 12.2, 13.9, 16.1])


X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=42)

model = LinearRegression()
model.fit(X_train, y_train)          # training: finds the best w (and a constant)
print(model.predict([[9]]))          # prediction for a house of size 9
print(model.score(X_test, y_test))   # how well it does on unseen data (1.0 = perfect)Classification and why accuracy can lie

Regression predicts a number, such as a price. Classification predicts a category, such as spam or not spam, or fraud or not fraud. The obvious score is accuracy, the share of predictions that are correct, but it can badly mislead you.

Real example: a bank has 1,000 transactions, and 10 are fraud. A lazy “model” that always says “not fraud” is right 990 times, so its accuracy is 99%. It has also caught zero fraud, which makes it useless.

Two metrics fix this. Call a case a true positive when the model flagged it and it really was the thing you’re hunting (spam, fraud).
